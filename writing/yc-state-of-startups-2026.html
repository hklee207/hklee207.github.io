What YC's batch data says about 2026

October 2, 2026 · 4 min read · Y Combinator, The Lightcone podcast

Y Combinator sees a lot of early companies, so what it chooses to fund is a decent early signal for where things are going. In "The State of Startups in 2026," an episode of its Lightcone podcast with Garry Tan, Jared Friedman, Diana Hu, and Harj Taggar, the partners walk through what changed in their last twelve to eighteen months of accepted companies.

One caveat up front. These are YC's own numbers about what YC chose to fund, and end-of-batch revenue is self-reported. I also read it through a transcript with auto-captions, so a few company names are garbled and I have left those out. The trends matter more to me than the names.

Bits to atoms

The headline stat is that hard tech, meaning companies working with physical things, went from about 8% of a batch to about 20%. Robotics went from roughly 1% to 6-7%. Industrial manufacturing from about 4% to 10%. Defense from about 1.5% to 5%. Semiconductors and photonics, and power infrastructure, each climbed from around 1% to 3-4%.

The explanation that I found most convincing was not "investors got bored of SaaS." It was that code generation lowers the software cost of building hardware. A company used to need something like a thousand strong engineers to compete with Google or Meta on a full hardware stack. Now it needs one or two, and that changes the economics. Frontier models also speed up the research itself.

They named three pulls: space, defense, and compute. On compute, one detail stuck with me: renting an A100 got more expensive even though the chip is old, because demand outruns supply. That pulls startups into every layer, from data-center build-outs and power to alternative chips and optical switching between GPUs. The example of a company rebuilding US metal manufacturing in empty Detroit factories, and growing at software rates because defense startups want a supplier that moves at their speed, made the supply-chain angle concrete.

The partners also flagged their own counter-signal. Investors rotated fast into hard tech when software stocks fell, then software recovered. So the lesson is not that software is dead.

Software that does the job

This was the part I found most useful. The share of companies doing full-stack, end-to-end work went from about 10% to over 25%. Median monthly revenue at the end of a batch, starting from zero, rose from around $8K to around $20K. Some companies went from nothing to seven figures within the three-month batch, which used to take about eighteen months.

The distinction is between software that helps a person track a task and software that carries out the task. Their example was a recruiting product that started as candidate search, then added an agent that contacts candidates and schedules interviews. They expect revenue per account to double or triple. The growth came from doing more of the job, not from adding seats. A human still does the part that needs judgment, like assessing fit.

There is also a warning for older products. A system of record has to become a harness, the place where work actually happens, or it becomes prey. Once its data can be pulled out through a connector, the switching cost collapses.

Data as a hidden business

Selling data and reinforcement-learning environments to AI labs was a tiny niche when YC funded Scale in 2016. In the last two years, they said, more than a dozen YC companies are each at $10M or more per year. Labs reportedly spend around $1B here, and the winners stay quiet. The physical version is starting too, with real-world footage for robotics.

Who starts companies now

Solo founders went from roughly 5% to 18-19% of accepted companies. The reasoning is that knowing what to prompt and what to build has become harder and more valuable than the ability to code the thing. But the partners were careful. Strong co-founders still raise the odds a lot, and the trend is to add them after traction.

The other shift is that experienced founders, in their late thirties through fifties, are back. The phrase I wrote down was that if you have been around the block, you know "where the dragons are." Managing coding agents looks a lot like managing people, so people with management backgrounds may run fleets of agents better than a smart nineteen-year-old. The partners presented that as an observation, not a promise.

What I'm taking away

If I had to boil it down to one idea, it is the one they said directly: you need to know what to build, and that is now the high-order bit. When building gets cheap, the scarce thing is judgment about which problem is worth solving. That judgment comes from lived experience in a domain, which is a human kind of moat and a different one from owning data.

It also fits with the consulting pieces I read earlier. Those said to sell outcomes, not inputs, and that proprietary data is the scarce input. YC adds the startup-side evidence: end-to-end companies are where the revenue jump is, and the data business is already large. Put the three together and the advice is to own an outcome, own a data position, or own the taste to know which one to pick.

Where I'm skeptical

Batch-share numbers describe what YC selected, not the whole market. Fast revenue at the end of a batch can include AI-hype spending, and the partners acknowledged some of it might be. A trend from a small, curated sample could look different at scale.

And I notice I want the "know what to build" story to be true, because it flatters people who have spent time in a domain. Their practical advice, to start prompting and to retry old problems with each new model, is at least cheap to test.
