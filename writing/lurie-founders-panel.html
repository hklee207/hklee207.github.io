Give First, Then Ask

September 29, 2026 · 4 min read · Founders panel, Lurie conference (Michigan)

This panel was a group of Michigan founders and startup leaders answering questions from undergrads and MBAs. It was less a keynote than a long, candid conversation, and it left me with a checklist.

How startups filter

One company requires a cover letter, and they throw out every application without one. The reason is that the letter shows whether you researched them. Do you know some of their customers? Do you know the founder's background? Do you believe in the mission?

That is a high bar only in the sense that almost nobody clears it. It takes an hour of reading.

The panel also had a clear view on what kind of person gets hired early: super-generalists or super-specialists. Specialists get hired at a particular moment, and generalists are welcome almost any time. The worst place to be is the middle, being described by others as okay at everything. A scalpel or a Swiss Army knife gets used. A butter knife that does everything slightly gets left in the drawer.

AI is assumed

Nobody gets hired for having AI on their résumé. It's assumed you'll figure it out. What counts is a thing you built. One panelist said it plainly: I don't care how you built it, just show me how it works.

Companies on the panel used AI throughout, but with rules. One was to do it manually first and then automate, so you know the process works before a model touches it. Another was to keep AI out of anything customer-facing that depends on authenticity. A founder's real face in an ad stays real.

Cold outreach works

This was the part I most wanted to hear. Founders answer cold messages. One said she answers all of them, including investors, and that some of her best investors came from cold outreach. A loose connection through Michigan is enough to get a reply.

What makes a message land:

  • A shared conviction. If it's clear you care about the problem, that matters a great deal.
  • An insight. One founder took a call because someone had spent two years on a similar idea that failed. The question was, what did you learn that I don't?
  • Rapport without an ask. Poke at their work, suggest something they should try. Founders find that fun.

The principle was to give first, and to expect one good response in 100. The story was about a grad student who offered an Italian soccer club a free study on where to put a new stadium, done as a class project. He got hired and eventually became CEO of AC Milan. The panelist said they recommend that approach every time. You hand over a free sample instead of asking for a job.

Writing compounds

One founder had written more than 100 blog posts. The first fifty were faking it. Then he became an actual expert. Only five to ten reached a wide audience, and roughly three readers were incredibly important, including one who became an investor.

I like the math there. Most posts do nothing. A few reach the right reader and are worth all the others. That is a decent argument for publishing a lot and not waiting until something is perfect, which is part of why this site exists.

Where to learn

On careers, the panel split on mission versus opportunity. Some said people stay at mission-driven companies for a decade because belief gets you past the hard days. Another became a CEO at 26 of a university spin-out less because of the subject and more because he believed in the co-founders and the technology. Both agreed that having mission and opportunity together is a narrow window.

The advice that stuck: go where you will learn the most. It's like picking the class with the great professor. Take it every time.

One panelist had planned to go into consulting to sample industries. A classmate warned her that she would go crazy, because on every project she would want to jump into the company and run operations. She interned at an early-stage company instead. That one landed hard for me. I'm weighing paths like that right now, and the test it gave me is simple: do I want to advise on operations or run them?

Founding, in short

  • Ship fast. Perfect is the enemy of good.
  • Founding is sales, to investors, hires, and customers. One founder takes sales and support calls every day because pivots come from there.
  • Write every change down with a date. Without a log it becomes mush and you can't tell what worked.
  • Choose investors more carefully than employees, because you can't get rid of them. Backchannel with their portfolio founders. One founder was told, by a CEO in an investor's portfolio, "you owe me a beer, do not take this investment."

What changes for me

  • Raise my bar for cold emails. Name their customers, reference the founder's background, tie it to their mission.
  • Make something to give. I'll offer a small, scoped piece of work to a company I care about before asking for anything.
  • Keep publishing. The volume is the point.
  • Start a talent list. One founder saved a name from an interview that didn't work out, and hired her years later. I'll keep my own list of people I'd work with, and add to it after every event.
  • Run the consulting test. Advise or operate. I'll answer it honestly before recruiting season, not after.
