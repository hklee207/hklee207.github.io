You Have More Power Than You Think You Do

September 24, 2026 · 4 min read · Dick Costolo and Fred Wilson, panel on Twitter, boards, investing and the AI wave (Michigan)

I went into this panel expecting Twitter stories. I came out with a better sense of how much of a company is just somebody deciding to act.

Match the founder's vision, not the playbook

Wilson told the story of how USV won the Twitter deal. Benchmark was competing, and their pitch was the standard one at the time: put Twitter on every phone and give the carriers a cut. USV said to forget the phone, put it on the web with an API, and let anyone build on top of it. That was what the founders wanted, so that was the deal.

You don't win people by running the common playbook harder. You win them by understanding what they are actually trying to build.

The room with 20 people in it

When Costolo became CEO, his board meetings had about 20 people in the room. Investors from several rounds, observers, extra partners from one firm. He opened one meeting with "welcome, board members, board observers, and uninvited guests."

His fix was almost boring. He went to the most reasonable investor, Wilson, who agreed to leave if he could sell a third of his shares. Another investor took the same deal. Eventually everyone was gone except one holdout, whom Costolo nudged out by threatening to move the meeting without telling him where.

His message to future founders was blunt: you have more power than you think you do. And someone has to fall on the sword first, because nobody goes until somebody shows the way. I think that applies well beyond boards. Most of the constraints in a company are social, and they hold only until someone calmly tests them.

Advertising is undefeated

Twitter's revenue was zero when Costolo arrived and about $2.2 billion when he left, as stated on stage. The idea behind it came from his years at Google: advertising is undefeated as a business model, because nothing else delivers that kind of revenue per user.

The twist was that Twitter couldn't copy Google's or Facebook's layout. Tweets flowed out through an API to apps Twitter didn't control, so an ad in a side panel would never travel with the content. The ad had to be a tweet. That is where in-stream ads came from, and Costolo said they did it out of necessity. Everyone copied it later.

I like this because the constraint produced the invention. The monetization worked because it was native to how the product was already distributed. It rode along with the thing people were already doing.

Reversing the strategy that won

The same open API that won the deal became a threat. Someone began buying up third-party Twitter clients with the aim of forking the network away from Twitter itself. Costolo restricted the API. It was, in his words, courageous and also massively vilified, and it saved the company.

A strategy that works can stop working. The hard part is being willing to reverse it while people are angry at you for doing so.

Hunters and gatherers

Wilson's investing lessons were my favorite part. It took him something like 15 years to get good. He said his aggregate return on deals shown to him over 40 years is negative, and the deals he went out and found himself, he crushed.

I think about that every time I'm tempted to wait for the right opportunity to show up in my inbox. What gets handed to you has usually been passed over by others. The things worth having tend to be the things you tracked down.

His Coinbase story fit the same pattern. He spent about a year and a half meeting every Bitcoin company he could find. They were either zealots or scammers. Then he met Brian Armstrong and found, in his words, a reasonable human being working in crypto. That was the whole thesis. In a messy market, the credible person is the product.

Late bloomers

The career advice was the part I didn't expect to stick. Wilson said many people in their late twenties sit in his office with law degrees, one or two years in, saying they screwed up and asking how to get out. They went to law school because their parents told them to. When asked how you find what you're good at, he said he didn't know, and suggested maybe working as a comedian for a few years. Costolo said it worked for him.

Costolo told a story about trying to recruit Jeff Bezos to Twitter's board. Bezos's line, in the middle of a conversation about saying no to things, was that there are lots of different ways to be successful. Costolo's own advice was the same: if you try to be someone you're not, it won't work.

What I'm taking from it

A few things I'll do differently.

  • Keep structure small early. If I ever build a company with outside investors, I want a small board from day one. Observers and guests are much easier to prevent than to remove.
  • Put monetization next to the existing behavior. The best revenue model is often the one that travels with what users already do.
  • Hunt. Cold outreach, my own research, going to find the thing. I will treat inbound lists as the weaker source.
  • Choose fit over what my peers are choosing. Costolo's path went from comedy to failed startups to a CEO job. It was not a straight line, and it did not need to be.
