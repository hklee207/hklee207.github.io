Consulting sold hours, and AI just priced them out

September 20, 2026 · 4 min read · Maurizio, Consulting Intel

I don't work in consulting. But I keep reading about it, because I think it is the clearest early view of what AI does to any job that is paid by the hour.

This one is a piece by Maurizio at Consulting Intel, a practitioner with about twenty years in the industry. It was published in April 2026 and I read it in September. His claim is blunt: for two or three decades, professional services ran on a craft model. Value was measured in headcount and billable hours. Under the strategy branding, a lot of it was body shopping, which means renting out human intelligence by the hour.

AI forces a move to what he calls an industrial model. You stop selling the brilliance of your people and start selling the precision of an AI-powered outcome. A firm that still pitches "30 analysts for 12 months to implement 3 SAP modules" is selling an input. Its client wants something else entirely.

The five tenets

He lays the transition out as five tenets. I'll give them in my own words.

  • Capability to outcome. Clients don't want an implementation. They want a re-imagined finance function or a self-optimizing supply chain. The firm should get paid for what it achieves, not for what it does, which means it has to carry real risk.
  • Codify expertise. Knowledge that lives in senior partners' heads and old decks is fragile. Firms need an internal engine so research, data cleaning, and first-pass synthesis cost close to nothing.
  • Redesign management. Separate strategy, data, HR, cloud, and ops teams are an artifact of the pre-AI world. Stitch them into one client interface with leaner teams and less friction.
  • Vertical over horizontal. AI is an excellent generalist. It summarizes, builds decks, and runs basic project management. The generalist coordinator is in trouble, and the moat is the specialist with deep domain knowledge.
  • Decouple scale from headcount. Replace the battalion of junior analysts with small pods: a couple of senior architects, a few AI-augmented specialists, a couple of juniors. If standard troops can do a task, it gets automated.

What I took from it

The line that stuck with me is that the analyst pyramid is a cost structure, not a value structure. I had always half-assumed the pyramid existed because the work needed that many people. The author's point is that it existed because that was how you billed for the work. Once software can do the work at near zero marginal cost, the pyramid has nothing holding it up.

The part I think generalizes is the fourth tenet. He says the horizontal coordinator is the first seat to be automated. That is uncomfortable, because a lot of junior product and program work looks exactly like coordination: herding a roadmap, running standups, writing the status update. If a model can do that well enough, then holding that job is not a strategy.

What survives, in his telling, is one of two things. You own a vertical, meaning you know one industry's workflows deeply. Or you own an outcome, meaning your pay is tied to a number you move. I now use that as a filter when I look at roles. I ask whether the seat owns a real number or a real domain. If it owns neither, I treat it as a warning sign no matter how good the brand is.

I also like how he ends. As the cost of executing complexity falls toward zero, he argues, the value of defining purpose rises. What stays scarce is the courage to pick the objective and the wisdom to handle what the change does to people. I find that more convincing than most "humans will always be creative" arguments, because it says specifically what humans are still needed for: deciding, and being accountable for the decision.

Where I push back

First, he is a consulting insider writing a manifesto, and manifestos flatten things. "Outcome pricing" sounds clean, but outcomes are often hard to measure and harder to attribute. A client will argue about whether the supply chain improved because of the consultants or despite them. Firms that take on outcome risk have to price that uncertainty, and I suspect many will quietly drift back to hours with a nicer name.

Second, "codify expertise" is easier to say than to do. The knowledge in a senior partner's head is often tacit. It is the ability to read a room, not a document you can feed to a model. I believe some of it can be captured, but I would not assume the zero-marginal-cost engine arrives on schedule.

Third, the piece is about firms, and I care about people. If pods replace pyramids, where do juniors learn? The pyramid was inefficient, but it was also how people got their reps. I don't have an answer, and the article doesn't offer one either.

Why I'm keeping it

Even with those caveats, the core idea holds up: the unit of sale is shifting from time to results. That is the same shift behind a lot of what I read about software and startups right now, and consulting is just where it shows up first because its product was always so close to pure labor.

For me it is practical. When I choose where to spend my next couple of years, I want to be near someone who owns an outcome and sits inside a specific domain, not in a role that supplies hours to a machine that is learning to supply them cheaper.
