I've moved between countries, schools and languages more than most people my age. For a long time that felt like starting over again and again. Looking back, each move left me with one thing I still use every day. This is the longer version of how I got here.

## Toronto, from age six

When I was six, my family moved from Seoul to Toronto. I didn't speak English yet. I learned it the way kids do, by being thrown into it: at school, on the ice, and on the slopes. Toronto is where I first played hockey and learned to ski, and hockey quickly became the thing I loved most.

![Me as a kid in ski gear](assets/journey/toronto-ski.jpg "Ski season in Canada.")

From Toronto we traveled across the States. Every trip was a lesson in how big the world was, and how differently people outside of Asia lived, worked and thought. I didn't have the words for it then, but that was the start of a habit I still have, which is to look at a place and ask why it works the way it does.

## Summer camps, and a competitive school system

Summers meant camps, many of them. They put me in rooms with kids from everywhere, all a little too serious about learning, and I loved it.

![At a Johns Hopkins summer program](assets/journey/camp-friend.jpg "A Johns Hopkins summer program.") ![With my mom](assets/journey/camp-mom.jpg "With my mom, who made all of it happen.")

Back in Korea, I grew up inside one of the most rigorous, competitive academic systems in the world. It taught me discipline and how to keep going under pressure. What it didn't do was make me give up hockey. I kept playing, and in 2015 I made the Korean youth national inline hockey team, where we took third place at the AAU International and I finished as top scorer.

Then something changed. Korea didn't have the environment to keep growing as a player, and at the same time math and science were pulling at me harder than anything else. I loved that a few rules could explain how so much of the world works. So I made my first real trade-off: I stepped away from the sport I loved for the subjects I wanted to spend my life on.

## Chicago: learning how to do research

Chicago was the first place I lived in the United States on my own. I started Industrial Engineering at the University of Illinois Chicago in 2022.

![Union Station, Chicago](assets/journey/chicago-union-station.jpg "Union Station. Chicago was my first home in the States.")

I wanted to do research, and nobody was going to hand it to me. I sent hundreds of cold emails, and most went unanswered. One reply was from Professor Dieff Vital, who took me into his lab. I worked on RF power harvesting, and the work was published in IEEE. More than any result, he taught me how research actually gets done: ask a narrow question, test it, be honest about what you found. Chicago is also where I met people who made the dream feel less abstract and more like a plan.

## Michigan, the first time

In January 2024 I transferred to the University of Michigan to study Industrial & Operations Engineering. IOE turned out to be the name for what I'd been circling for years: using math to decide how people, machines and resources should be organized.

## Eighteen months in the Army

After my sophomore year I flew back to Korea for mandatory military service, like every Korean man does. I served 18 months as a logistics officer and interpreter, from August 2024 to February 2026.

At first it felt like my life was on pause while everyone else kept moving. One night after a shift, I looked around and realized everyone beside me carried the same quiet weight. I wasn't alone in it. A line from Viktor Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning* kept coming back to me: the last human freedom is to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances. I couldn't change where I was. I could choose how I stood inside it.

![With my unit](assets/journey/army-unit.jpg "With the people who taught me what unity means.") ![Joint training with US Army medics](assets/journey/army-joint-training.jpg "Joint training with US medics. Interpreting between the two sides was part of my job.")

So I treated the two hours a day that were mine as my one chance to build. Over 18 months that added up to about a thousand hours. I finished Andrew Ng's Machine Learning Specialization, passed CFA Level I as an engineer who had never studied finance, and won Michigan's Hydrogen Grand Challenge with my teammates. I also met a friend on a training range, ran into him again in an entrepreneurship class, and together we reached the finals of the Ministry of National Defense's start-up competition. That team is now building [Apeul](https://apeul.com). The gap I had feared became the most formative season of my life.

## Seoul, and seeing my work matter

Two months after I was discharged, I joined GLG Korea as a project associate intern. The role was finding and screening experts for clients. But I kept noticing how much of the work was slow and manual, so I built tools for it, including the [Value Chain Explorer](../posts/value-chain-explorer.html), and presented them to the Korea leadership.

![The Han River at sunset](assets/journey/seoul-han-river.jpg "Seoul.") ![A street corner in Japan at night](assets/journey/tokyo-street.jpg "Japan, August 2026.")

That summer taught me what kind of work I want. I want to see the change I'm making, in front of me, with real people using it. A skill that has never been tested against a real problem is only half learned.

## New York, again

Seventeen years after my first trip, I walked through Central Park again. Same city, very different person. As a kid I was amazed by how big everything was. This time I kept thinking about how it all works: who decides, who pays, who builds.

![Central Park Reservoir and the skyline](assets/journey/nyc-reservoir.jpg "The Reservoir, August 2026.") ![The Beresford over Central Park](assets/journey/nyc-beresford.jpg "Central Park West.")

## Now: Ann Arbor

Today I'm back at Michigan as a junior. I'm doing research with Professor Wentao Weng on how AI and humans should share work, an operations research question: when should an AI decide on its own, and when should it hand the task to a person whose time is limited? It joins everything I've cared about: math, engineering, and the people on the other side of the system.

At the same time, I'm watching the world change, closely and every week. My dad sends me his report on the global economy every Tuesday and a market note almost every trading day, and we argue about them. That conversation became [Worldview](../worldview/index.html) and [Tinkerly](https://tinkering-weekly.vercel.app).

For the past year I've been trying to do two things at once: build skills that let me change something real, and understand the world well enough to know what is worth changing. Toronto taught me the world is big. Hockey taught me to commit. Chicago taught me to ask. The Army taught me to choose. Seoul taught me to build. Michigan is where I'm putting them together.
