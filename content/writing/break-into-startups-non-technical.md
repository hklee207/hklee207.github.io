This is another essay, not an event. I'm including it because it gave me a vocabulary I kept using for weeks afterward.

The author, Chris, went from early-stage VC to growth and marketing at a Series A startup. Seven months in he had been promoted, contributed to real closed revenue, and been noted by Clay as a go-to-market engineer to watch. His point is that a non-technical person can break in and move fast. He's still figuring it out, and he says so.

## Building or selling

His starting simplification is that at most startups you are either building or selling. An early company needs exactly two things: build something people want, and figure out how to get people to buy it. Engineers have an obvious path on the first. If you're non-technical and early in your career, he says to get as close as possible to the second.

Later-stage startups have plenty of other roles, such as product, finance, operations, recruiting, and chief of staff. But those get easier to enter after a few years and a clearer skill set. For someone just out of school, the easiest way to be valuable is to work on the thing the company cares about most, which is revenue.

That can mean selling directly, generating pipeline, producing content that drives traffic, running events, or automating any of it.

## Outbound and inbound

The titles are a mess. Sales, growth, community, demand gen, SDR, BDR. His clean split:

- **Outbound** is you going to find the customer. Decide which companies should buy, find the right people, start the conversation. That includes prospecting, account research, list building, and outreach. The typical titles are SDR and BDR.
- **Inbound** is making customers find you. Content, video, blog posts, landing pages, SEO, events, case studies. The typical roles are founding marketer and community.

Growth, he says, isn't a third thing. It means you sit somewhere between the two, or do everything, depending on the company.

I've started reading job posts through this lens, and it helps a lot. Half of the confusing titles collapse into one of the two columns.

## GTM engineering

The part I found most useful is his definition of go-to-market engineering: using software, data, AI, and automation to make sales and marketing more effective. You don't need an engineering degree.

His examples are concrete. A team manually researches 100 accounts, and you build a workflow that researches 10,000. You manually find the right person at each company, and you build something that handles it. Marketing repeats the same research weekly, and you automate it. A few years ago that needed engineers. Now tools like Clay and AI coding agents let non-engineers build surprisingly capable systems.

He is careful about what this does and doesn't mean. Not being an engineer doesn't mean being afraid of technical things. You don't need production-grade code, but you should know what GitHub is, be comfortable in the terminal, understand your file system, and roughly know how APIs work. AI does more of the implementation. Your job is to understand the problem well enough to specify it, give the right context, recognize when the output is bad, and steer it.

That last skill is taste. His formula for a non-technical person entering startups now:

> first-principles critical thinking + basic technical literacy + taste

## What it means for me

I'm studying industrial engineering, and I write code and run agent tooling most days. By his standard I'm already past the technical-literacy bar. What I hadn't done was connect that to a specific seat.

The seat I keep landing on is the one between the market and the product. I want to be the person who understands customers well enough to shape what gets built. His argument is that early on, the credible entry point to that seat is the revenue side, not a product title. Product roles open up after you have reps. That changed my plan. I used to think about getting to product directly. Now I think about earning the right by owning a number that matters to the company first.

A few practical things follow.

- **Build one real workflow.** I want to learn Clay well enough to build an actual enrichment and outbound system, as a portfolio piece and not a tutorial.
- **Use the vocabulary.** When I describe my own projects' go-to-market, I'll say outbound and inbound, which is clearer than the vague words I used before.
- **Keep reading the series.** This post is part one. The next ones cover how to pick the startup and how to get noticed, and I'll be reading them.

There is an open question I don't have a good answer to. He says product is harder to break into early. Is that still true for someone who has already shipped things and watched real users? I'll revisit it once I've had more time on the revenue side.

This essay pairs well with the founders panel I went to later in September, where people kept saying the same thing from the other direction: show me something you built, and do work before you ask for a job. Both say that the way in is to produce evidence.
