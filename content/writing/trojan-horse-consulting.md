A few weeks after the piece I wrote about earlier, the same author, Maurizio at Consulting Intel, published a sequel. The first said consulting had to industrialize or die. This one, from May 2026, says the industrialization may be a hostile takeover in disguise. I read them as Part 1 and Part 2.

The framing device is the Trojan Horse. The labs, with OpenAI and Anthropic as the Greeks, are at the gates of the Fortune 500. The people paid to guard those gates, the elite consulting firms, pulled them inside.

## The setup

According to the piece, in February 2026 OpenAI announced what it called Frontier Alliances: multi-year, multi-billion-dollar partnerships with the big strategy and accounting firms. The firms would not just use the software. They would embed OpenAI's forward deployed engineers directly in client teams. On the surface that looks like a win for everyone. The author cites Accenture booking about $2.2B in AI-related work in a single quarter.

His worry is what sits underneath. Consultants hold something Silicon Valley lacks: access. They have the contracts, the NDAs, the trust of the C-suite, and above all the proprietary data buried in old ERP systems and mainframes. By partnering to implement AI, he argues, they may be hand-delivering the training data the labs need.

Why would the labs need that? He leans on the Stanford AI Index from April 2026, which he says confirmed that high-quality public human text is largely used up. If that is right, the next gains need new material: the logic of a merger, the nuance of a supply chain disruption, how a global bank actually runs. Every time a consultant runs an AI agent on a client's operations, the agent is helping and learning at once.

## Three stories that cannot all be true

I liked how he lays out the three players and what each believes.

- The consultant says AI augments the firm. The firm sells the idea, AI handles execution, and the seat at the table stays permanent.
- The AI lab says consulting is high-cost, low-scale friction. The goal is an autonomous enterprise that needs an API, not a quarterly partner review.
- The client says it cares about profit. If the technology gives answers without a $500 hourly rate, the client will go around the firm.

Those can't all come true, and the client's story is the one with the money behind it.

He also describes an innovator's dilemma that I thought was well put. To stay competitive, the incumbents must ship the best AI tools, or an AI-native boutique will. But the better those tools get, the more they erode the billable hour underneath them. He adds the idea of "ghost firms," AI systems that do an analyst class's work for the price of some tokens, with a caveat I appreciated: whether that is really cheaper is unknown until token prices settle, and tokens are as cheap now as they will ever be.

## What I'm taking from it

The idea I care about most is not the drama. It is the structural claim that proprietary workflow data is becoming the scarce input, and that whoever sits inside the customer's messy systems controls it.

That has a practical consequence for how I evaluate companies. A business that sits on top of someone else's model API has little to defend. A business that lives inside a customer's workflow, and collects the data that workflow throws off, has a position. I now try to ask of any company which of those two it is.

It also makes the forward deployed role look less like a niche title and more like a wedge. If the labs are buying access by embedding engineers, then the person who sits with the client and builds with them is doing a job that mixes selling and product in one seat. That is the shape of work I find most interesting.

## Where to be careful

I want to be honest about the limits here, because the author is an insider with an incentive to dramatize, and I don't want to repeat a good story as fact.

The three hard claims, the February alliances, the Accenture number, and the Stanford finding, are what the argument stands on. I read them through his newsletter, not through the primary sources, and I have not yet checked them. I would treat them as claims to verify before I cite them anywhere that matters.

The thesis also has a leap in it. Even if consultants are feeding the labs data, it doesn't follow that the labs capture all the value. Clients have contracts and legal teams, and they will fight over who owns what comes out of their systems. The question I would rather ask is who ends up capturing the value of enterprise data: the labs, the integrators, or the clients themselves. I don't think anyone knows yet, and I suspect the answer varies by industry.

And "2027 will remember this as the moment the gatekeepers gave away the keys" is a line written to be quoted, not a forecast I would bet on.

## Why it stays on my list

Read together, the two pieces make one argument: the old consulting model is being priced out, and the new one is not guaranteed to be better for the people inside it. What I take as lasting is simpler and more useful. Own the workflow, own the data position, or own an outcome. A thin layer on top of someone else's system is the weakest place to stand.
