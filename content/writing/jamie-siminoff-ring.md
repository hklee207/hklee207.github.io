Jamie Siminoff built Ring, a video doorbell company, and sold it to Amazon for about $1 billion in 2018. He later returned to Amazon to oversee Ring and related products. His book is called *Ding Dong*, and this fireside chat covered it. What I liked most was that he did not sound like a founder who had it figured out. He sounded like someone who kept running.

## A chip on the shoulder

He started with childhood. Around 15 he wasn't good at sports and spent a lot of time alone, tinkering in the basement. Being picked on, being not quite normal, gave him a fire to build something big that people would see. He said he definitely has a chip on his shoulder.

## Speed is cycles

His main idea was a way of thinking about speed. He borrowed a line from *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid*: I shoot better when I move.

His model is that life and business run in cycles. Each cycle is one thing you launch and learn from, like firing a rocket. The question is how many cycles you can complete and how fast you can make each one. Each turn gets you further ahead of someone else, whether you are competing or trying to deliver something that saves lives.

Then the line I wrote down: the biggest opportunity in the world is to find people who are turning their cycles slowly, and turn yours faster.

That changed how I look for markets. I used to look for big or growing ones. Now I also ask where the people in a market are slow, because slow cycles are where a fast newcomer wins.

## Unreasonable on purpose

The interviewer, a VC who has known him since around 2012, called great entrepreneurs the unreasonable person. Siminoff took it as a compliment.

His version: a reasonable person faces a wall and turns around. An unreasonable person puts their head down and runs at it. Sometimes the wall really is impassable and you explode, and in entrepreneurship you go back to zero and start again. Often you somehow get through, then find more walls. Ring had four or five moments where it was done, plus small walls nearly every day.

He was honest about the cost. Entrepreneurship is lonely because every decision is yours. He described getting to 3 a.m. in tears. He said he kept going partly because the mission mattered, and partly because he was too all-in to leave. He said he would not recommend that, but it was real. In the end, he said, he sold because he couldn't take it anymore.

I appreciated that. A lot of founder talk implies a calm, endless fortitude. He said even the people you'd call legends don't have it.

## Fear as fuel

His fear was that someone would leapfrog Ring. He imagined himself as a bartender telling customers he was the inventor of the video doorbell, and them answering that no, the other company was. Inventing something and not being the one to get it out there was the thing he couldn't stand.

That fear drove aggressive marketing and a market position he called unnatural for a company of Ring's size. I'd never thought of fear as something you can point at a specific target and use. Vague fear is paralyzing. Specific fear is a plan.

## Lead by what people see you do

His view of leadership is that it comes from people seeing what you are willing to do. He worked the floor at a Best Buy over a Black Friday weekend, selling product. He put his email address on every box and told customers he was available to anyone, anytime. If the founder is available, everyone else has to be.

He dislikes all-hands meetings because they are usually not genuine. A founder says work hard and then leaves for a luxury hotel. What inspires people is hearing that the founder is in the office building a new product, answering customers, and upset that something is broken.

I think this is correct and underrated. Culture documents don't make a culture. Behavior does, and people copy the behavior at the top.

## Sell the solution

He said Ring was built around one focus, efficacy, meaning making neighborhoods safer, instead of sales budgets. Everyone else in home security built products and nobody cared much about whether they worked. His thesis was that if you make people safer, they will reward you by buying. Humans don't buy products, he said, they buy solutions.

## Should you drop out?

A student asked a version of this, given how much AI startup energy there is. His answer used a fingerprint analogy: from far away they all look the same, but up close each one is unique. It depends on the person. He needed the four years with other people. Some people don't. The real danger is copying: don't do something because you saw someone else do it. Advice is terrible if you copy it.

That is the line I'd keep from the whole event. I can learn from every panel I attend without mimicking any of them.

## What I'm doing with it

- **Measuring my own cycle time.** For each project I'll track how long one build, launch, and learn turn takes, and try to shrink it.
- **Looking for slow markets.** Places where people still run things by hand or with the wrong tools are the ones I want to study.
- **Describing outcomes, not tech.** When I pitch something, I'll lead with what changes for the person.
- **Being reachable.** Early users should be able to reach me directly, the way his email on the box did.
