The Tariff Wall and the Fed's Turn

October 28, 2025 · 2 min read

The first reports covered two moves that pointed in opposite directions. At the end of August, Powell described the labor market as a "curious balance" and treated tariff pass-through as a one-time price jump rather than lasting inflation. That opened the door to a September rate cut. In the same week, Washington took a stake of nearly 10% in Intel. Money was getting easier, and the state was stepping further into the economy.

What happened

An appeals court then ruled that the emergency law behind the reciprocal tariffs did not authorize tariffs at all. I expected a retreat. Instead the administration began rebuilding the wall under a national-security clause, Section 232. Cause: a legal loss. Effect: a different legal route to the same policy. The tariffs were never just a bargaining chip.

While Washington argued about legality, the bond market gave its first verdict. In early September, long-dated debt sold off in several countries at once. UK 30-year gilts hit 5.7%, Japan's 30-year reached a record, and the US 30-year sat near 5%. Britain was being called a canary for indebted rich countries, and France lost a prime minister over a budget.

The Fed cut on 17 September anyway. Investors responded in a new way. They kept their American assets but bought protection against the dollar. The phrase going around was "Hedge America." Nobody was leaving, but everybody was hedging.

What Dad emphasized

Dad kept returning to the gap between politics and prices. Leaders can announce what they like, but debt has to be financed every week by people who can say no. He also wanted me to notice that tariffs and state ownership are two faces of the same idea: the government as an active player in the economy, not a referee.

What I take from it

As a future founder, I take away that policy risk is not a one-off. A rule struck down in court can come back in a new form within days, so a business plan that depends on one tariff rate surviving is fragile.

As a future investor, I take away that you should watch what money does, not what officials say. Hedging is a quiet signal. It tells you trust has not collapsed, but it has gotten conditional.

I also learned to read a ruling in two steps. First, what did the court decide? Second, what will the government do next? The second question mattered far more than the first.
