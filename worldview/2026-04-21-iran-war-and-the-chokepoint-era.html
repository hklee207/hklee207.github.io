The Iran War and the Chokepoint Era

April 21, 2026 · 2 min read

The theory I first heard in October became something I could watch in real time.

What happened

On 28 February, the US and Israel began striking Iran. By 1 March, Khamenei was dead, and there was no clear exit strategy. Iran's answer was the chokepoint. The Strait of Hormuz was closed in practice, three ships were hit in a single day, and WTI touched $119. A record 400-million-barrel release from emergency stocks barely moved the price. Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex declared force majeure, taking roughly a fifth of the world's liquefied gas offline.

Analysts started saying freedom of the seas was ending. The loop that had recycled oil money into dollar assets for decades also broke, because the cash flows depended on ships that could no longer pass safely.

A ceasefire came on 7 April. The damage did not stop with it.

What Dad emphasized

Dad generalized the lesson. In economic war, chokepoints beat tariffs. He said every country now wants what he called strategic indispensability, a kind of mutually assured economic destruction. To say "don't cut off what we need," you have to be able to say "we can cut off what you need."

He also stressed the ironies. The war strengthened China's green industrial complex, and it sped up the clean-energy shift that the US administration had been trying to slow. The old ESG label was being replaced by something more like Economics, Security, Geopolitics.

What I take from it

For a founder, resilience is a product feature now. Customers want to know what happens when a shipping lane closes, and companies that can answer that will have an edge over those that cannot.

For an investor, I learned to look at second-order winners. A war in one region shifted the competitive position of an entirely different country. The headline loser and the real loser are not always the same.

I also learned to be careful with my own timeline. A ceasefire is a date on a calendar, but supply chains, insurance rates and trust take much longer to recover.

The deeper habit I am building is to ask who holds the narrow point everything passes through, and what they would do with that leverage.
